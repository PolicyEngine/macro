About the benchmarks

No change is the naive baseline: the last outturn at the round's data edge, held flat (read from the current vintage, so revisions can shift it slightly). This is the driftless walk; against a walk with drift the CPI advantage largely evaporates (see the limits below).

Published paths were fixed earlier, on less data: the BoE MPR in February, the OBR EFO in March and the Bank's survey of external forecasters (SEF) in April 2026, against July rounds. They show where views differed, not who forecasts better.

Why publish a record this sparse

A handful of scored forecasts is not an accuracy headline. Publishing from day one makes the sparse early record, and any failures, part of the evidence.

How we score: six rules, each enforced in code
  1. Archive firstThe round's JSON is committed before the outturn is published.
  2. Never editCI rejects any edit or deletion; a correction is a new round.
  3. No hindsightA round archived after the outturn's snapshot is never scored on it.
  4. Dated outturnsRead from a dated ONS snapshot; revisions are appended, never overwritten.
  5. Error and coverageSigned error of the median; coverage of the bands as archived.
  6. BenchmarksBeside a no-change forecast and any published path for that quarter.

Archive and freeze. A round is one JSON file per model under forecasts/rounds/<date>/, holding the median and the 68% and 90% bands for every quarter, the data edge (the last observation the model saw), and the hash of the artifact it was copied from. The forecast-archive workflow fails any pull request that edits, renames or deletes one, so the Git timestamp is the proof of when the claim was made.

No hindsight. score.py scores a period only when the round's archive date is on or before the date of the snapshot the outturn was read from; a later round that also covers the quarter stays in the archive but is never scored on it. The test is by date, not by the publisher's release time. The one round where that difference matters is the first, archived after the CPI release from a file committed before it; the archive says so.

Outturns. ingest_outturns.py converts the stored ONS series to the forecasts' units (GDP to year-on-year growth from the ABMI level; CPI and unemployment as published) and appends each value with its snapshot date. When the ONS revises a quarter a second value is appended; the scoreboard uses the newest, and every earlier score stays reproducible from outturns.json.

Errors and bands. Errors are the archived median minus the outturn, so a positive error means the forecast ran high; they are signed because a steady bias and random misses are different problems. Band coverage is scored on the bands exactly as archived, not on the re-calibrated bands drawn in the open-round charts.

Benchmarks. The minimum bar is a no-change forecast: the last outturn at the round's data edge, held flat. Where a stored quarterly path exists, the Bank of England's February 2026 MPR central projection, the OBR's March 2026 EFO and the Bank's April 2026 survey of external forecasters are shown beside it, from the committed official CSVs. Everything lands in scorecard.json, which is generated, never edited by hand.

Limits

What this record does not show.

A few scores prove little
No skill claim until the scored count is well into double figures, at least a year away.
The bands run too narrow
In hindsight testing both the 68% and the 90% band cover less than they should, worsening with horizon; the open-round fans below are re-calibrated (coverage study).
Unemployment bands understate uncertainty
By construction: they carry GDP-forecast uncertainty only, and the satellite’s own residual is not sampled, as its round file states.
CPI does not beat a fair naive benchmark
Against a random walk with drift the advantage largely evaporates, and no accuracy gain survives correction for the number of tests run.
Three variables, no fixed calendar
GDP growth and CPI from the BoE SVAR, unemployment from its satellite.
About these limits

Of the variables tested, Bank Rate comes closest to an accuracy gain, and it is not forecast here yet. Rounds follow data-edge refreshes rather than a schedule, and coefficients are not re-estimated every round.

Comparing with the EFO

The EFO was fixed in March and these rounds in July, so the charts show where the views differ, not who forecasts better; that evaluation is on the BoE SVAR validation page. A round archived after an outturn is drawn beside it but never scored on it.

Band calibration, and where the quarter-by-quarter numbers are

The GDP and CPI bands are rescaled per horizon to the coverage measured on the validation page — which widens some and narrows others: averaged across variables the bands under-cover, but UK GDP over-covers at short horizons, so its 68% factor is 0.86 at one quarter ahead and 1.44 at eight (factors in band_calibration.json). Those factors are measured on the model that is actually shown, with the six Covid dummies; the earlier set came from a coverage run estimated without them, and narrowed the GDP fan by 36% at one quarter ahead where the published model wants 14%. They are quoted to four decimals because that is the arithmetic of the run, not the precision of the estimate — 49 overlapping origins carry a standard error that moves k far more than its fourth decimal. The raw quantiles stay untouched in the round artifacts. The quarter-by-quarter numbers are in the round artifacts below; the BoE MPR central path and the external forecasters' survey are in the committed official CSVs.

Append-only archive and scoring inputs

A CI job fails any pull request that edits or deletes a round, so a correction creates a new round and the superseded one stays scored. Once scored, a card shows how each claim fared.

Why the store exists

Rounds are scored against official data, and official data gets revised. Dated snapshots let a score be reproduced against the data as it stood, not as it was later revised. Reading only the current value would make look-ahead bias undetectable, because only one version of the past would ever exist.

Read a series as it was published on a date

Take the newest snapshot on or before your date. No key, no account, standard library only. 

import json, urllib.request

BASE = "https://policyengine-macro.vercel.app/data"

def get(path):
    with urllib.request.urlopen(f"{BASE}/{path}") as response:
        return json.load(response)

def as_of(source, series, date):
    dates = get("MANIFEST.json")["series"][series]["vintages"]    # every snapshot ever taken
    dates = [d for d in dates if d <= date]
    if not dates:
        raise LookupError(f"no {series} vintage on or before {date}")
    return get(f"vintages/{source}/{series}/{max(dates)}.json")   # immutable file

cpi = as_of("ons", "uk_cpi_yoy", "2026-07-25")
print(cpi["vintage"], cpi["observations"][-1])                    # 2026-07-25 {'period': '2026Q2', 'value': 2.8}

Two dates, two snapshots: the difference between them is the revision. The store starts 2026-07-25 and runs forward from there — it does not reconstruct vintages older than itself.

Endpoints and file format
Static files over HTTPS, GET and HEAD only — there is no API to authenticate against. Everything under /data/ is served with Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *, so a browser-side notebook or dashboard on any origin can read it directly.
EndpointContainsStability
/data/MANIFEST.jsonIndex of every series: source, CDID, units, frequency, coverage, and every snapshot date.Rewritten on each fetch; short cache.
/data/latest/<series>.jsonThe newest snapshot, flattened. Same schema as a vintage file.Moves. Do not cite as a fixed reference.
/data/vintages/<source>/<series>/<YYYY-MM-DD>.jsonOne dated snapshot, exactly as fetched.Immutable by construction; one-year cache.
/data/calendar.icsThe release calendar above, as iCalendar.Regenerated with the store.

Each file carries series, source, cdid, title, frequency, units, url, release_updated, first_period, last_period, observations, vintage, fetched_utc. Snapshot dates are when a change was recorded, not a daily calendar: a fetch that found nothing new wrote no file. ONS and Bank of England series are under the Open Government Licence v3.0; US series come via FRED with their original BEA, BLS and Federal Reserve attribution.

About the engine and the macro members

The engine underneath this section is pe-microsim — PolicyEngine's own microsimulation, the only member whose question types include household and the only one that covers both countries. It applies a reform to household microdata and reports taxes, benefits, net_income, revenue, distribution over a single policy year. The only two members that report a distribution are pe-microsim and psl-og+microsim, and the second is an overlay that runs the first.

The macro members are what it cannot do on its own. pe-microsim cannot answer GDP, inflation, interest rates and macro feedback — so every macro number in this section comes from a member that produces one, and the table says which, in the order they run. Four of the six routes put pe-microsim in the chain; two are a macro model standing alone, which is also a fine thing to publish.

pe-microsim’s question types are household, population, policy_reform: forecast is not among them, and its predictive validation is recorded as not_applicable. The uncertainty of boe-svar is posterior 68% and 90% intervals.

pe-microsim does not forecast, and this section does not pretend it does. The forecast rows are boe-svar; the scoreboard above is what those rounds have been worth so far. See how a score is put together →
