\section{Comparison with the reference implementation, the published record and other models}
\label{sec:comparison}

\subsection{The Fed's pyfrbus and the published FRB/US record}
\label{sec:cmp-published}

This section consolidates the paper's headline numbers against the published record in one place. Three kinds of comparison must be kept apart, and the first of them is not a comparison at all. A number that is \emph{true by construction} --- an identity, a calibration input reported as an achieved target, a solver tolerance reported as accuracy --- can never fail, so it belongs in the table only if it is labelled as such where it appears. The tracking residual of Section~\ref{subsec:tracking} is the case in point and is flagged in the table below rather than counted as agreement. The second kind is \emph{like-for-like}: the Fed's own \texttt{pyfrbus} reference implementation runs on this machine, on the same \texttt{model.xml}, the same LONGBASE vintage and the identical shock, so the two implementations should agree --- and they do, to solver precision. Re-running the Board's demo experiment (a 100-basis-point add-factor to \texttt{rffintay} under surplus-ratio fiscal targeting) in both codes gives a maximum absolute difference of $6.0\times10^{-9}$ across all 284 endogenous variables and all twenty quarters, i.e.\ solver-tolerance noise. This one is real evidence: the shock moves the solution away from the tracked baseline, so nothing forces the two codes to agree. The third kind of comparison is against \emph{published} FRB/US output --- most usefully the Board's own simulation of the same funds-rate shock in the current (post-2018) model under VAR expectations \citep[Fig.~2]{laforte2018changes} and the cross-model fiscal ranges of \citet{coenen2012effects} and \citet{whalen2015fiscal}. There the numbers can only be close, not identical: the published simulations were produced on earlier model and database vintages, from different baselines, and with shock designs (e.g.\ persistence of the funds-rate innovation) that the notes do not fully pin down. Table~\ref{tab:published-comparison} shows both kinds side by side.

\subsection{Recent Federal Reserve work with FRB/US}
\label{sec:cmp-recent}

Beyond the FEDS Notes, the more recent Board literature provides two further, if less directly numerical, checks. First, \citet{brayton2022linver} build their linearised LINVER model precisely around the experiment run here --- a 100-basis-point shock to the inertial Taylor rule --- and report goodness-of-fit statistics of $0.999$--$1.000$ between LINVER and full FRB/US for the output-gap, inflation and funds-rate responses to that shock; the impulse-response \emph{shapes} they plot (hump-shaped activity decline, small inflation response) are the shapes in Figure~\ref{fig:mp100}, though the paper quantifies magnitudes only graphically. Second, the framework-review literature quantifies how much the expectations option matters, in a way that brackets our VAR-only results: \citet{hebden2020makeup} report that, relative to full model-consistent expectations, inflation expectations respond to policy news two years ahead by approximately zero under VAR expectations, 40 per cent with model-consistent financial markets only, and 80 per cent when wage--price setters are also model-consistent. Our implementation sits at the ``approximately zero'' end of that spectrum by construction, which is why forward-guidance-style exercises are out of scope (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) and why our funds-rate-shock responses should be (and are) at the large end of the published range. \citet{kiley2025anchoring}, the most recent Board research use of FRB/US at the time of writing, likewise runs its policy-rule comparisons across expectations settings rather than publishing a canonical funds-rate IRF; no 2024--2026 Board publication does, leaving \citet{laforte2018changes} and LINVER as the reference points used above.

\subsection{Situating FRB/US among other U.S.\ benchmarks}
\label{sec:cmp-crossmodel}

A different question from implementation fidelity is where FRB/US's responses sit relative to \emph{other} models and to the reduced-form evidence. Table~\ref{tab:crossmodel} collects benchmarks for the effect of a 100-basis-point policy-rate shock on activity. Three caveats govern the reading. The response variable differs (GDP here; industrial production, a far more cyclical series, in most of the VAR literature); the shock designs differ (our shock decays with the inertial rule, VAR shocks are typically one-off innovations); and the CBO row is deliberately a non-comparison --- CBO's published interest-rate ``rule of thumb'' holds GDP fixed by design and prices out only the budgetary cost, so it cannot be read as a macro response at all.

The placement is comfortable: FRB/US under VAR expectations delivers activity effects at the moderate end of the empirical range --- above the smallest CEE-style VAR estimates, close to the post-1984 FAVAR evidence once industrial production's greater cyclicality is taken into account, and well below the ``medium effects'' narrative-identification numbers --- with a peak-effect timing (about two years) shared by nearly every entry. The NY Fed DSGE attributes a somewhat larger per-shock output effect but the same flat-Phillips-curve property of small inflation responses. On the fiscal side, the CBO comparison is already embedded in Table~\ref{tab:published-comparison}. CBO's published ranges (0.5--2.5 for federal purchases; 0.3--1.5 for two-year tax cuts for lower- and middle-income people and 0.1--0.6 for a one-year cut for higher-income people) are cumulative over several quarters and assume that the Federal Reserve did not tighten \citep[Table~3 and its note~a]{cbo2015arra}, so they cannot be set directly beside a calendar-year ratio under the Taylor rule. The purchases comparison therefore uses the quarterly response paths of \citet[Table~2]{whalen2015fiscal}, converted to this paper's definition: all four purchases multipliers lie inside the converted ranges, in their lower part. CBO publishes no central tax-cut multiplier. The 0.3 that an earlier version of this paper called one is the low end of the two-year lower- and middle-income row, in the no-tightening regime \citep{frbbenchmarks2026}; our year-two 0.32 sits between that row and the higher-income row, under a different monetary regime. The FRB-US lines in \citet{coenen2012effects}, read from their charts, are higher than ours: by about 0.2 in year one with no accommodation and about 0.3 in year two with two years of accommodation. Model vintage (theirs is the 2009--10 FRB-US) and expectations mode (which their paper does not state) could each account for a gap of about that size.

\subsection{Reading the comparisons}

The like-for-like agreement is the decisive test: on identical inputs the two codebases are the same model to solver precision, and against the Board's own simulation of the current model the published gaps are small: the core-PCE trough agrees to $0.001$pp and in the same quarter, the output-gap trough is $0.02$pp deeper and the unemployment peak $0.04$pp higher \citep{laforte2018changes}. The larger inflation response in the 2014 FEDS Note ($-0.08$pp) describes the pre-2018 model; \citet{laforte2018changes} attributes the smaller current response to ``the re-estimation of the wage-price block equations over a longer sample period that now extends through 2017''. Figure~\ref{fig:cmp-multipliers} summarises the fiscal comparison graphically, Figure~\ref{fig:cmp-irf-feds} overlays the published peak responses on our impulse responses, and Figure~\ref{fig:cmp-pyfrbus} shows the ours-versus-pyfrbus overlay, in which the two lines are visually indistinguishable.
