\section{Calibration and validation}
\label{sec:validation}

Validation is against ground truth --- the restrictions and published figures of \citet{brignone2025} --- rather than the mere absence of exceptions. The checks fall into three tiers: exact invariants that must hold on every draw, sign checks on quantities the identification does \emph{not} restrict, and quantitative comparisons with the paper's reported magnitudes inside deliberately wide bands that respect the replication's proxy data and Monte-Carlo noise. All checks run in continuous integration on the real dataset.

\paragraph{Exact invariants --- checks that cannot fail, and are reported as such.} On \emph{every} accepted identification draw: (i) each element of $B$ restricted to zero in Table~\ref{tab:restrictions} is zero to machine precision (largest violation below $10^{-9}$); (ii) every sign restriction holds strictly ($s \cdot B_{vj} > 0$); (iii) $BB' = \Sigma$ within $10^{-7}$, so every accepted $B$ is a valid structural factorisation. None of these three can come out any other way, and the numbers should be read accordingly: the zeros are imposed by the \citet{arias2018} construction, the signs \emph{define} acceptance (a draw violating one is discarded, so the pass rate over accepted draws is one by definition), and $BB'=\Sigma$ is the factorisation identity the construction starts from. What they test is that the implementation does what the algorithm says --- a floating-point and indexing check on the code, not evidence that this replication reproduces the Bank's model. The residuals quoted ($10^{-9}$, $10^{-7}$) are solver tolerances, not accuracy against any external quantity. The one check in this group that carries information is the last: median impact impulse responses also respect every Table~\ref{tab:restrictions} sign, which is not guaranteed draw-by-draw acceptance and could in principle fail.

\paragraph{Unrestricted sign checks.} The paper's key overidentifying check is that the real oil price --- whose response to a world supply shock is left \emph{unrestricted} --- rises after a positive world supply shock. The replication reproduces this: the median impact response of the sterling real oil price to a one-standard-deviation world supply shock is $+5.83$ ($100\cdot\log$ units), positive as in the paper's Figure~2.

\paragraph{Comparison with published magnitudes.} Table~\ref{tab:validation} collects the model-versus-paper comparison. The headline comparison is the share of UK forecast-error variance attributed to the identified global shocks (world demand $+$ energy $+$ supply) at the one-year horizon, where the paper reports roughly 40 per cent for UK GDP and roughly 50 per cent for UK CPI.

Getting that comparison like-for-like took three corrections, all made in August 2026 and all of which had been inflating the replication's agreement with the paper. First, the \emph{horizon}: the decomposition of equation~\eqref{eq:fevd} accumulates squared moving-average coefficients, so its column $h$ holds the variance of the $(h{+}1)$-step-ahead forecast error. ``One year'' is therefore column 3, not column 4; reading column 4 was reading five quarters. Second, the \emph{statistic}: the shares were formed by summing the six identified shocks' pointwise posterior \emph{medians} and renormalising the result to 100 per cent. The per-shock medians sum to about 0.62 (GDP) and 0.71 (CPI) here, so that renormalisation inflated every identified share by roughly a third. Third, the \emph{target}: the paper's Figure~4 decomposes ``the mean of the sum of the decomposition'', and its note attributes the residual to 100 per cent to the unidentified shocks --- its text states that the six identified shocks explain around 80 per cent of the domestic variables, of which the global shocks are around 40 per cent of real GDP and 50 per cent of CPI variation one year after the shocks. Its shares are therefore posterior \emph{means} of shares of \emph{total} forecast-error variance, renormalised over nothing.

Computed the paper's way --- the group share formed on each accepted draw, averaged, four quarters ahead --- the 10{,}000-draw production run gives \textbf{37.4} per cent for UK GDP against the paper's $\sim$40, a match, and \textbf{42.3} per cent for UK CPI against $\sim$50, about eight points short. The previously published pair, 42.1 and 49.5 per cent, appeared to match on both; the CPI agreement was the renormalisation, not the model. What the shortfall does \emph{not} support is a strong claim in either direction: the 68 per cent posterior band on these shares is $[23.4, 52.2]$ for GDP and $[25.5, 60.3]$ for CPI, roughly $\pm14$ points, wider than the eight-point gap being discussed. The identified shocks in total explain about 76 per cent of UK GDP and 79 per cent of UK CPI one-year variance here, against the paper's ``around 80 per cent'' --- so the disagreement is about the \emph{split} between global and domestic, not about how much the six shocks explain.

The $[30, 60]$ per cent acceptance band used by the per-commit test suite should be read for what it is: a regression tripwire, not a calibration result. A band 30 points wide around targets of 40 and 50 admits values from three-quarters below to half again above the published number, so ``in band'' is close to a claim that cannot fail; it is set to catch a gross code regression without going flaky on Monte-Carlo variation and the proxy world aggregates, and passing it is not evidence of agreement. The informative comparison is the point estimate against $\sim$40 / $\sim$50 with its posterior band, above.

Nor do the importance weights explain the remaining distance. The cheap unweighted CI configuration (600 draws, 51 accepted) gives 42.0 and 37.6 per cent, but it is a noisy estimator --- across sampling seeds its global GDP share has a standard deviation of about 6.5 points at that draw count --- and the unweighted 3{,}000-draw run behind Figures~\ref{fig:irf}--\ref{fig:hd} gives 38.1 and 42.3 per cent, essentially the weighted production values. The gap between the CI configuration and the production run is therefore Monte-Carlo noise in the accepted sample, not the weights correcting the \citet{arias2018} sampler towards the uniform-over-rotations posterior; an earlier version of this paper attributed it to the weights.

\paragraph{Internal consistency.} Identities are verified to numerical precision rather than assumed: FEVD shares sum to one at every horizon on every draw within $10^{-10}$ (equation~\ref{eq:fevd}); the historical decomposition~\eqref{eq:hd} --- structural shocks plus Covid component plus deterministic component --- reconstructs the observed data within $10^{-8}$, and the stochastic component equals the sum over shocks within $10^{-10}$; and in the forecast-revision exercise the identity ``forecast at $T$ $=$ pseudo-forecast at $T{-}1$ $+$ composite impulse response'' holds per draw with a maximum absolute error of $5.3\times10^{-12}$ across all draws, horizons and variables.

\paragraph{Rolling-origin forecast evidence: no demonstrated skill.} A separate
expanding-window pseudo-out-of-sample evaluation estimates the reduced-form
BVAR at 49 historical origins, at horizons one to eight, and compares its
posterior-mean path with three benchmarks: no change (a driftless random walk),
a random walk \emph{with drift}, and an AR(1). No structural restriction or
FEVD target enters this exercise. Read carelessly it is the most flattering
evidence in this paper; read properly it is the least.

Three things have to be got right before any of its ratios mean anything, and
until August 2026 this section got two of them wrong.

\emph{The benchmark.} A driftless random walk on a trending log level is a weak
opponent, and most of the apparent skill is against it: CPI-level relative RMSE
is 0.63 at one quarter and 0.67 at eight (values below one beat the benchmark),
while UK GDP is worse than no change at both (1.06 and 1.06). Against the
drifting walk the CPI advantage shrinks to 0.83 at one quarter and reverses to
1.03 at eight. Figures from the no-change columns must not be quoted inside a
drift-benchmark discussion --- the ex-Covid UK GDP ratio of 0.77 and the UK GDP
$p$-value range of 0.38--0.67, both of which have circulated, are no-change
numbers; against drift the same quantities are 1.21 and 0.33--0.43.

\emph{The specification.} The evaluation had always estimated \emph{without} the
six Covid dummies (2020Q1--2021Q2) that every published forecast in this paper
carries, so it scored a model nobody publishes. Re-run on the published
specification, UK GDP against drift goes $1.06 \to 0.99$ at one quarter and
$1.12 \to 0.95$ at eight --- level with the naive benchmark rather than worse,
and still not better. CPI moves by less than 0.02, so its weakness at long
horizons is not a Covid artefact. Both blocks are committed; the
production-specification block is the like-for-like one.

\emph{Multiplicity.} The exercise runs $8$ variables $\times\ 8$ horizons $= 64$
Diebold--Mariano tests per benchmark, so the smallest of them is not a
discovery. Under a Benjamini--Hochberg false-discovery-rate adjustment across
the whole grid, the minimum $q$-value against the drifting random walk is
\textbf{0.36} in the headline block and \textbf{0.40} under the published
specification: \emph{nothing clears a 10 per cent false-discovery rate on any
variable at any horizon}. Bank Rate comes closest --- relative RMSE 0.78--0.87
against drift and an unadjusted $p = 0.018$ at one quarter, the smallest of the
64 --- and does not survive. Only against the weakest benchmark, no change, do
any $q$-values fall below 0.10 (two in the headline block, three under the
published specification), and none below 0.05.

The honest summary is therefore narrower than the earlier one: this model has
\emph{not} demonstrated forecast skill against a drifting random walk on any
variable at any horizon, and the seven-quarter frozen-edge result of
Section~\ref{sec:results} is a single origin, not a contradiction of that. The
generated evidence is committed as
\texttt{figures/rolling\_evaluation.json} alongside this paper and as
\texttt{results/rolling\_evaluation.json} in the model repository, with its
\texttt{production\_spec} and \texttt{multiplicity} blocks.

\subsection{Interval coverage across rolling origins (added July 2026)}
\label{sec:coverage}

A companion evaluation, added in July 2026 and committed as
\texttt{coverage\_evaluation.json}, asks whether the predictive \emph{bands}
are calibrated, not just whether the point forecasts beat benchmarks. Using
the same expanding-window design, the reduced-form BVAR is re-estimated at 49
historical origins; at each origin, 100 posterior draws with 5 stochastic
paths per draw generate predictive distributions, and percentile bands on the
level of each series are confronted with the subsequent outturns at horizons
one to eight.

The bands under-cover. Averaged across the eight variables and eight
horizons, the nominal 68 per cent band contains the outturn 62.1 per cent of
the time and the nominal 90 per cent band 77.2 per cent of the time, and
coverage worsens with horizon: the 68 per cent band covers 71 per cent at one
quarter but only 57 per cent at eight quarters, the 90 per cent band 87 per
cent at one quarter and 72 per cent at eight. Three caveats bound the
reading: the Covid quarters are included with no Covid dummies in the
evaluation model; estimation uses final revised data, so the exercise is
pseudo- rather than real-time; and consecutive origins overlap, so the
effective number of independent observations is well below 49. None of these
caveats reverses the direction of the finding --- users should treat the
published credible bands as too narrow, especially at longer horizons.

\subsection{Replication figures}
\label{sec:repl-figures}

Figures~\ref{fig:irf}--\ref{fig:hd} show the replication's core structural outputs, generated by \texttt{figures/make\_figures.py} from a fixed-seed run of 3{,}000 posterior draws (241 accepted; unweighted, which as shown above moves one-year FEVD shares by well under a point relative to the importance-weighted production benchmark).

Figure~\ref{fig:irf} plots impulse responses to the three shocks that carry most of the narrative weight. The world demand row shows the classic demand comovement --- world GDP, oil prices, UK CPI and UK GDP all rise --- with the UK CPI response building over two to three years. The world energy row shows the expansionary (disinflationary) energy shock: activity up, oil and UK CPI down, with the oil-price response large on impact ($-6$ to $-7$ in $100\cdot\log$ units) and persistent. The UK monetary policy row shows a tightening lowering both CPI and GDP with the familiar hump; its world-variable responses are exactly zero on impact by construction and small thereafter, the small-open-economy assumption at work. Only the impact signs in Table~\ref{tab:restrictions} are imposed; the persistence of every response is untouched data information.

Figure~\ref{fig:fevd} stacks the FEVD shares for UK GDP and UK CPI by horizon. The pattern behind the comparison in Table~\ref{tab:validation} is visible directly: at the one-year horizon (marked) the three identified global shocks account for 38.1 per cent of UK GDP variance and 42.3 per cent of UK CPI variance in this unweighted run --- within a point of the weighted production figures --- with the world demand share of CPI growing steadily in the horizon and the unidentified shocks retaining about a fifth throughout. Domestically, UK supply is the largest identified contributor to GDP variance --- and, in this replication's unresolved discrepancy with the paper's prose, also to CPI variance, where the paper says monetary policy contributes most.

Figure~\ref{fig:hd} gives the historical decomposition of year-on-year UK CPI inflation from 2016 --- the model's structural account of the 2021--23 inflation surge and its aftermath. The surge is attributed overwhelmingly to global shocks: contractionary world energy shocks and strong world demand dominate the 2022 bars, with UK-specific shocks and the Covid-dummy component playing supporting roles, and the decomposition unwinding through 2023 as energy shocks reverse. This reproduces the qualitative account in the source paper's Figure~6 and matches the Bank's contemporaneous narrative of imported inflation.

\paragraph{Narrative reproduction.} The estimated shock series reproduce the paper's episodes: the 2008 collapse in world demand, the contractionary world energy shocks of 2022, UK monetary loosening readings after 2022 followed by tightening readings in 2023, and muted shocks during 2020--21 by construction of the Covid dummies. Known caveats are equally documented: median UK GDP responses to world demand and supply shocks decay through zero at long horizons where the paper's remain positive (the paper's path lies inside the replication's bands), and within the global supply side the energy shock contributes more than the non-energy supply shock to world GDP and oil-price variance, where the paper's figure suggests the reverse ordering; the paper's claims about the \emph{combined} supply side are matched.
