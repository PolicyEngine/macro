OBR emulator

How the published EViews listing becomes a model you can solve and shock.

Overview Methodology Validation Code Paper

Transpile, solve, swap the closure

The pipeline in six steps: transpile, initialise, solve, anchor, shock, and join to the PolicyEngine microsimulation.

More detail

The OBR's published EViews equation listing is rewritten in Python, initialised from official data, solved quarter by quarter until the equations agree, anchored so the baseline reproduces the published EFO, and shocked against an identical baseline so the difference is the policy effect. The microsimulation supplies the static costing the shock comes from.

1

Transpile the published listing

The OBR publishes its full equation listing, but as EViews source that few can run. The transpiler rewrites every EViews line — dlog(), @elem, lags like X(-1), error-correction terms — into an equivalent Python expression, compiled once per solve.

About this step

What comes out

The result is the published listing in open code, runnable without an EViews licence. All 372 equations parse, but it is not a complete working copy of the OBR’s model: about 79 are skipped at solve time because inputs they need are not published, some headline variables pass through at their published values rather than being computed, and business investment uses an equation the OBR had commented out of its listing, reconstructed here.

2

Initialise from official data

History loads from the OBR's detailed forecast tables. Of the 261 endogenous variables the listing referenced but the outlook did not print, 199 had ONS series codes; a vendored 355-series ONS snapshot supplies them.

Data sources
Forecast tables
OBR detailed forecast tables, March 2026 EFO
ONS snapshot
355 vendored series; 199 of 261 unprinted variables matched
About this step

Why it matters

That snapshot ships inside the package — there is no download step — and it gives the circular fiscal and financial sub-blocks real balancing data rather than guessed starting values.

3

Solve by Gauss–Seidel iteration

The equations depend on each other, so each quarter is solved simultaneously: cycle through the equations, update, repeat to convergence. Quarters then solve sequentially forward.

Solver
Within quarter
cycle → update → repeat to convergence
Across quarters
sequential forward solve
About this step

Runtime

A twelve-quarter anchored solve takes roughly 30–40 seconds single-threaded; a reform run is two such solves.

4

Anchor to the published EFO

Add-factors — the residuals reconciling each behavioural equation to data — are set so the baseline reproduces the published EFO. This is the OBR's own device, used the OBR's own way.

Anchoring

to 2031Q1 (fit on the validation tab)
About this step

Forecasting with held add-factors

For forecasting rather than one-off shocks, the emulator fits add-factors over a base window of 2024Q1–2025Q4, holds them constant, and projects to 2027Q4, with exogenous inputs extrapolated (seasonal cash series at their trailing annual mean). Scenario solves run twelve quarters, 2025Q1–2027Q4.

Horizon limits

The anchored horizon extends to 2031Q1; how closely it holds there, and where unemployment stops being usable, is on the validation tab.

5

Swap the closure, shock, compare

In the OBR file GDP (GDPM) is an exogenous input and inventories (DINV) are already the residual that closes the expenditure identity.

Closure and shock
Closure swap
DINV equation removed and held at EFO; GDP identity added, GDPM endogenous
Policy effect
Xtshocked−Xtbaseline, identical structure
About this step

Closure swap

For a shock the emulator removes the DINV equation, holds DINV at its EFO path, and adds a GDP identity so that GDPM is endogenous — output responds to the policy instead of being fixed.

Shock against an identical baseline

The emulator solves shocked and baseline paths with identical structure and reports the difference, isolating the policy effect from the underlying forecast.

6

Score a costed reform through the microsimulation bridge

The suite's flagship join.

The bridge
Injection
HHDI_ADDFACTOR = −(annual costing) × 1000 ÷ 4, £m/qtr, held
Transmission
HHDI → RHHDI → CONS → GDPM
Command
pe-macro score --country uk --reform '{…}' --model obr
About this step

Static costing in, second-round effects out

PolicyEngine's microsimulation costs a statutory reform year by year — who pays and how much — and score_reform(country="uk", reform=..., model="obr") hands this model the bill: each year's costing enters as HHDI_ADDFACTOR, minus the annual £bn spread flat across its four quarters (×1000 ÷ 4 → £m/qtr), held as an add-factor on household disposable income.

What the emulator computes

Lower income moves consumption through the published consumption equation, and consumption moves GDP under the demand closure — the second round, quarter by quarter, against the anchored baseline of step 4. The anchoring add-factors are held fixed in both the baseline and the shocked run, so they cancel in the difference. The worked example on the Overview shows what this returns for 1p on the basic rate; individual quarters are not reliable — no quarter of the solve converges to tolerance.

Where the bridge stops

The household route’s multiplier is close to the OBR’s published figure for income tax; the by-construction problem belongs to the spending lever (CGG), not to this one (limits). Corporation tax is refused — not household-borne in the microsim, and the direct TCPRO closure converges only slowly. UK only; no US bridge exists. Every join in the suite → combine the models.
