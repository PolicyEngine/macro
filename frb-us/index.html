About this step

The Federal Reserve Board has used FRB/US for policy analysis and forecasting since 1996 and publishes it whole in the public domain: the equations (model.xml), the LONGBASE database, the documentation and a reference solver, pyfrbus.

This build parses the Board’s own file rather than re-specifying it: 284 endogenous variables and their equations, solved as one simultaneous system per quarter on numpy, scipy and sympy. In the suite it is the US counterpart of the OBR emulator: an estimated system an official institution relies on, not behaviour derived from first principles like the overlapping-generations model. Unlike the OBR emulator, it has no bridge from statute. How it is solved →

About this step

One command, pe-macro frbus-shock --var rffintay_aerr --shock 1.0, adds 1pp to the inertial-Taylor-rule error term and returns the quarter-by-quarter paths for the funds rate, real GDP, unemployment and core inflation. What the microsimulation join (step 3) takes in is the labour-market path, summarised above as the 2027 wage bill (compensation per hour plus hours, pl + lhp).

The response itself, set against the Board’s documented simulation properties, is the worked shock on Validation; fiscal multipliers are there too.

About this step

frbus_shock_incidence scales the US microsimulation’s employment income with the wage and hours deviations a shock produces, so the automatic stabilizers answer who bears it: how much of the earnings loss federal and state taxes absorb, and what SNAP and the EITC give back, decile by decile.

Uniform scaling is an experimental illustration: real incidence concentrates in job losers. The join runs one way only. score_reform refuses model="frbus" rather than return a plausible-but-wrong number, because no mapping exists from a PolicyEngine reform to FRB/US levers.
