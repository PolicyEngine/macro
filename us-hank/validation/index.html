The parameters it solves for

The ground truth is the published paper, not data.

About this step6 points
  • β 0.9762739 against 0.976, and χ1 6.4164196 against 6.416. χ1 is solved numerically from a 6.5 starting guess, the strongest single check.

Known limitations

LimitDetail Not a forecaster

No data, no estimation, no forecast origin: every result is an impulse response around the paper's calibrated steady state. Nothing in this suite forecasts the US.

Stylised instruments only

Monetary, fiscal-spending and productivity shocks; the labour tax is endogenous. No transfer or tax-rate instrument, so score_reform refuses model="hank".

First-order responses

Hosted IRFs are linear in shock size: no state dependence, no zero lower bound, and a rise mirrors a cut. The model itself is not linear: on the nonlinear transition, a spending rise of 1% of GDP (ρ 0.8) gives an impact multiplier of 0.251; a cut of 1% of GDP lowers output by 0.125% (a multiplier of 0.125), against the first-order 0.175. A 25bp quarterly (≈100bp annualised) monetary cut is close to linear: 0.511 against 0.493. At +2% of GDP the nonlinear solve does not converge. Parameter uncertainty is not quantified — the calibration is a single point.

MPCs below the empirical range

The aggregate quarterly MPC is 0.058 out of the default windfall, 25% of mean quarterly post-tax labour income (about $2,400 per head or $6,200 per household). On a refined liquid grid, rebates of $500, $1,000 and $1,500 per household give 0.104, 0.091 and 0.083, far below the 0.15–0.25 that US rebate studies find. The grid explains little of the gap. The main cause is the illiquid account’s adjustment cost: it is smooth and convex, with zero marginal cost at zero adjustment and no fixed or linear component, so a constrained household that holds illiquid wealth puts most of a rebate into that account. Auclert et al. (2021) report no MPC, so this is a property of the calibration, not a replication failure (us-hank-model #6, PR #7).

Hand-to-mouth share too high

About 54% of households (0.537) hold under the hand-to-mouth cutoff of liquid wealth, against about one third in Kaplan, Violante and Weidner (2014). Almost all of it (0.518) is wealthy hand-to-mouth, against about two thirds in the data. The share is high while the MPC is low: most of these households hold illiquid wealth. The paper reports no hand-to-mouth share, so this is this implementation’s own statistic.

No hump-shaped responses

After a monetary shock, output, consumption, investment and inflation all peak on impact; empirical monetary responses are usually delayed and hump-shaped. The model has no habits or information frictions to delay them.

The real rate is ex-post

The reported r is the ex-post real return on bonds held into the quarter. On impact it moves only with the inflation surprise; the policy move shows from quarter 1. For the 25bp quarterly (≈100bp annualised) cut (ρ 0.61) it falls 0.08pp on impact and 0.18pp in quarter 1.

Spending multiplier turns negative

A spending rise of 1% of GDP (ρ 0.8) raises output by 0.175% on impact, but output is below baseline from quarter 3 (impact is quarter 0) while spending is still raised; the 20-quarter cumulative multiplier is −0.015. The labour tax that finances the spending enters the union wage Phillips curve: a higher tax raises the wage unions set, which raises inflation, and the Taylor rule answers with a higher real rate that crowds out investment and consumption. The household and government budget constraints hold along the path, so this is a property of the model, not a bug (tests/test_spending_mechanism.py; us-hank-model #5). No published impulse response gates these numbers. It is a tax-financed multiplier, not comparable with deficit-financed ones. The sign depends on the MPC: in the opt-in 0.20-MPC calibration the multiplier is 0.376 on impact and 0.309 over 20 quarters (below).

Persistence of a spending shock

The hosted hank_shock has no default ρ for a spending shock: persistence must be given (monetary and productivity shocks default to 0.9). The model’s README quotes ρ 0.8. For the same spending shock the impact multiplier is 0.075 at ρ 0.9 and 0.175 at ρ 0.8, and the 20-quarter cumulative multiplier is −0.032 against −0.015. Quote ρ with any multiplier.

Opt-in calibrations

Two opt-in calibrations ship in the model package (us_hank.beta_het) but are not the default and are not hosted. A discount-factor heterogeneity variant is calibrated to a 0.20 MPC out of $1,000: spending impact multiplier 0.376, 20-quarter cumulative 0.309 (ρ 0.8). A median-wealth sensitivity gives a $500 MPC of 0.126 and multipliers of 0.274 / 0.213 (PR #9, PR #10, #8). No result on this site comes from them.

Approximate distributional output

By-quartile consumption splits the aggregate response across total-wealth quartiles in proportion to steady-state MPCs — the MPC-heterogeneity channel, not household-level dynamics along the transition. The split is nearly flat: for the 25bp quarterly (≈100bp annualised) cut, 0.36% in the lowest quartile and 0.28% in the highest.

Calibration, not vintage

The parameterisation is the paper's; there is no data vintage to refresh. Changing it would be a model change.
